Recordings become data that can be searched and analysed. For audio, speech-to-text produces transcripts with timestamps, diarisation labels who spoke when, and language identification and translation handle mixed-language content. For video, sampled frames are analysed to detect objects, faces, on-screen text, scenes and actions, often with descriptions generated by multimodal models. A contact centre, for example, transcribes every call and then extracts topics, sentiment and required compliance phrases.

Typical uses are call analytics, meeting search, media archives, safety and quality inspection, and training data for AI. The work is compute-heavy, often needs GPUs, and costs grow with hours of media rather than rows. Accuracy depends on recording quality, accents, specialist vocabulary and camera conditions. Recordings of people raise questions of consent, retention and biometric data, and face and voice recognition are regulated in many jurisdictions.

# Key considerations

- Test transcription accuracy on your own recordings, including specialist terms and accents, before choosing an approach.
- Keep timestamps that tie extracted text and labels to the moment in the recording, so people can verify them.
- Redact personal data such as card numbers from transcripts and audio where required.
- Size compute by hours of media per day, and decide what can run in batch rather than live.

# Questions to ask

- How many hours of audio or video arrive each day, and how quickly are results needed?
- Which languages, accents and specialist vocabulary must be handled?
- What consent and retention rules apply to the recordings and to any biometric features?
